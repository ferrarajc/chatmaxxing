@page fund-groups-explained | How the lineup is organized
@summary What distinguishes the four fund groups and the style categories within U.S. equity.

## The four groups

- **U.S. Equity**: funds holding stocks of U.S. companies. They differ by company size (large, mid, small), by style (growth, value, blend), and by special focus (dividends, ESG screening).
- **Sector Equity**: funds concentrated in one of the eleven sectors of the U.S. economy, such as technology or utilities. Narrower and usually more volatile than broad funds.
- **International**: funds holding stocks of companies outside the United States, broadly (BFIN, BFDEV) or by region (BFEUR, BFPAC) or in emerging markets (BFEMG).
- **Fixed Income**: bond funds, differing by issuer (U.S. Treasury, corporate, or a broad mix) and by maturity (short, intermediate, long).

## Size and style

U.S. stock funds are described with a "style box": size (Large, Mid, Small) and style (Value, Blend, Growth).

- **Growth** funds hold companies whose earnings and sales are expected to grow faster than average; they tend to have higher prices relative to earnings and lower dividends.
- **Value** funds hold companies that look inexpensive relative to earnings, book value, or dividends; they tend to pay higher dividends.
- **Blend** funds hold both.

Large companies are generally more established and less volatile; small companies offer more growth potential with more volatility. These are general characteristics, not predictions.

## Total market versus 500

BF500 holds the 500 largest U.S. companies. BFTM holds the whole U.S. market, about 3,650 companies, including mid and small caps. Because the largest companies dominate both by weight, their returns are usually similar. See [[fund-bf500]] and [[fund-bftm]].

## Bond funds by maturity

Shorter-maturity bond funds (BFST, BFSTC) are less sensitive to interest rates and usually yield less; longer funds (BFLTT, BFLTC) are more sensitive and usually yield more. Treasury funds carry no credit risk; corporate funds carry credit risk and usually yield more. See [[bonds-and-interest-rates]].

@page nav-pricing | NAV: how fund prices work
@summary How a mutual fund's net asset value is calculated, when it is set, and why it differs from stock and ETF pricing.

## What NAV is

A mutual fund's price is its **net asset value (NAV) per share**: the total value of everything the fund owns, minus its liabilities, divided by the number of shares outstanding. Every buyer and seller on a given day transacts at that day's NAV.

## When it is set

NAV is calculated once each business day after the New York Stock Exchange closes at 4:00 PM ET, usually published by 6:00 PM ET. Orders received in good order before 4:00 PM ET receive that day's NAV; orders received later receive the next business day's NAV. See [[trade-cutoff-nav]].

## Differences from stocks and ETFs

Stocks and ETFs trade continuously during the day at changing prices. Mutual funds do not: there is no intraday price, no bid-ask spread, and no limit order. A client who asks "what price will I get?" should hear that it will be the NAV calculated at today's close (if before the cutoff), which is not known in advance.

## Why NAV drops on distribution dates

When a fund pays a dividend or capital gain distribution, the NAV falls by the amount paid out on the ex-dividend date. The client's total value is unchanged: they have fewer dollars in the share price and the same dollars in cash or reinvested shares. See [[ex-dividend-dates]].

## Dollar-based orders

Because the NAV is not known in advance, most clients buy and sell in dollars. The number of shares is calculated at the NAV, to three decimal places. A $1,000 purchase at a $218.40 NAV buys 4.579 shares.

## Where to find NAV

The fund's Research page shows the latest NAV and the daily change. Historical NAVs appear on the price history chart. Clients' trade confirmations show the NAV of each transaction.

@page expense-ratios | Expense ratios
@summary What an expense ratio is, how it is charged, the range across our lineup, and how to explain cost to clients.

## Definition

The expense ratio is the annual cost of operating a fund, expressed as a percentage of the fund's assets. It covers management, administration, custody, and other fund costs. A 0.03% expense ratio means $3 per year for every $10,000 invested.

## How it is charged

The expense ratio is **not** billed to the client's account. It is accrued daily inside the fund and reflected in the NAV. The returns we publish are already net of expenses. This is why a client never sees a line item for it on a statement.

## Our range

BobsFunds expense ratios range from **0.03%** (BF500, BFTM, BFBI) to **0.13%** (BFREI). Sector funds are 0.09%. See [[fund-lineup-overview]] for every fund.

## Common questions

### "How much am I paying in fees?"
Multiply each holding's value by its expense ratio. A client with $40,000 in BF500 pays about $12 a year. There are no other account fees unless they use a paid service such as an outgoing wire. See [[fee-schedule]].

### "Why did the expense ratio change?"
Expense ratios are updated in each annual prospectus and can go up or down slightly as fund assets and costs change. The current prospectus figure applies.

### "An old page says BFGR costs 0.25%."
Some older client materials listed higher expense ratios for the original funds. The current prospectus figure (shown on the fund's profile and Research pages) is authoritative. Apologize for any confusion and report the outdated page.

@page risk-levels | Published risk levels
@summary The five risk levels shown on fund pages, what they are based on, and how to discuss them without advising.

## The scale

Every fund page shows one of five risk levels: **Low, Low–Medium, Medium, Medium–High, High**. The level reflects the fund's historical volatility and the nature of its holdings.

| Level | Typical funds | General characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| Low | BFST | Short-term government bonds; small price movements |
| Low–Medium | BFBI, BFITT, BFSTC, BFSTP, BFUTL | Intermediate bonds or defensive stock sectors |
| Medium | BF500, BFTM, BFIN, BFDEV, BFLTT | Broad diversified stock funds; long Treasuries |
| Medium–High | BFGR, BFMC, sector funds such as BFFIN, BFLTC | More concentrated or more rate-sensitive |
| High | BFSC, BFSCV, BFSCG, BFMCG, BFTEC, BFENE, BFEMG, BFDIS | Small companies, single sectors, emerging markets |

## What it is not

The risk level describes the fund, not whether it suits the client. Every fund, including the Low-risk fund, can lose value. A risk level is not a prediction.

## How to discuss it

Quote the risk level and explain what it means in general terms. If the client asks "is this too risky for me?", that is a suitability question. Say: "I can tell you how this fund is rated and how it has behaved historically; whether it fits your situation is something a licensed professional can help you decide." See [[advice-decline-scripts]].

## Standard deviation and beta

Fund profiles also show standard deviation (how much returns have varied, annualized over three years) and beta (how much the fund has moved relative to the S&P 500; 1.0 means in line, above 1 means more, below 1 means less). These are historical statistics.

@page benchmarks-explained | Benchmarks and tracking
@summary What a benchmark index is, why index funds track one, and why fund returns differ slightly from their benchmark.

## Benchmarks

A benchmark is an index that represents the market segment a fund invests in, such as the S&P 500 Index for BF500 or the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Float Adjusted Index for BFBI. Index funds aim to match their benchmark's return; the benchmark is also the yardstick for judging the fund.

## Why a fund's return differs from its benchmark

- **Expenses**: the index has no costs; the fund does. A fund with a 0.03% expense ratio will typically trail its index by about that much.
- **Cash and trading**: funds hold small amounts of cash for redemptions and incur trading costs.
- **Sampling**: bond index funds and very broad stock funds often hold a representative sample rather than every security.
- **Timing**: distributions and the treatment of dividends can cause small differences over short periods.

The gap is called **tracking difference**. For our index funds it is usually very small.

## Benchmarks for each fund

Each fund's profile page lists its benchmark. Older documents for the original six funds listed different benchmarks (for example, Russell 1000 Growth for BFGR); the current prospectus benchmark is authoritative.

## Example

In 2025 BF500 returned 12.6%, while the S&P 500 Index returned slightly more, the difference being mostly the fund's 0.03% expense ratio and small trading costs.

## Benchmarks for sector funds

Sector funds track "25/50" indexes, which cap individual company weights to meet diversification rules for registered funds. That's why a sector fund's largest holdings may be slightly smaller than in an uncapped sector index.

## Facts you can compare

When a client asks you to compare funds, use only published characteristics:

| Characteristic | Where to find it |
|---|---|
| Objective and holdings | Fund profile "What the fund does" |
| Category and group | Fund profile, lineup table |
| Expense ratio | Fund profile |
| Risk level, standard deviation, beta | Fund profile |
| Calendar-year returns | Fund profile; trailing returns on the Research page |
| Yield and distribution frequency | Fund profile |
| Duration (bond funds) | Fund profile |
| Number of holdings, sector allocation | Fund profile |

## How to present a comparison

Present the facts in parallel and let the client draw conclusions:

"Here's how they compare. BF500 holds about 500 large U.S. companies, costs 0.03%, and is rated Medium risk. BFGR holds about 235 large growth companies, costs 0.04%, and is rated Medium–High; it has had bigger swings in both directions, for example +46.8% in 2023 and −33.2% in 2022, versus +26.3% and −18.2% for BF500."

## What not to say

- "This one is better."
- "I'd go with..." or "Most people pick..."
- "Growth funds always beat the market over time."
- Any forecast of future returns.

## When the client asks you to choose

Offer a callback with a licensed professional. Use the Fund Comparison wrap-up code if the chat was mainly a comparison, or Investment Advice if the client was referred. See [[advice-decline-scripts]].
