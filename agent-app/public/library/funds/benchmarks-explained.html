Benchmarks and tracking

Section: Fund Lineup · Page ID: benchmarks-explained

What a benchmark index is, why index funds track one, and why fund returns differ slightly from their benchmark.

Benchmarks

A benchmark is an index that represents the market segment a fund invests in, such as the S&P 500 Index for BF500 or the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Float Adjusted Index for BFBI. Index funds aim to match their benchmark's return; the benchmark is also the yardstick for judging the fund.

Why a fund's return differs from its benchmark

  • Expenses: the index has no costs; the fund does. A fund with a 0.03% expense ratio will typically trail its index by about that much.
  • Cash and trading: funds hold small amounts of cash for redemptions and incur trading costs.
  • Sampling: bond index funds and very broad stock funds often hold a representative sample rather than every security.
  • Timing: distributions and the treatment of dividends can cause small differences over short periods.

The gap is called tracking difference. For our index funds it is usually very small.

Benchmarks for each fund

Each fund's profile page lists its benchmark. Older documents for the original six funds listed different benchmarks (for example, Russell 1000 Growth for BFGR); the current prospectus benchmark is authoritative.

Example

In 2025 BF500 returned 12.6%, while the S&P 500 Index returned slightly more, the difference being mostly the fund's 0.03% expense ratio and small trading costs.

Benchmarks for sector funds

Sector funds track "25/50" indexes, which cap individual company weights to meet diversification rules for registered funds. That's why a sector fund's largest holdings may be slightly smaller than in an uncapped sector index.
