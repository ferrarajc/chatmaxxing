How conversions are taxed

Section: Roth Conversions · Page ID: conversion-taxes

The taxable amount, the pro-rata rule, withholding, and reporting forms for conversions.

On this page

  • Taxable amount
  • The pro-rata rule
  • Withholding
  • Reporting
  • Effects beyond income tax

Taxable amount

The amount converted is taxable as ordinary income in the year of the conversion, valued at the fair market value on the conversion date. If shares are converted in kind, the value on that date is used.

The pro-rata rule

If the client has made nondeductible (after-tax) contributions to any Traditional IRA, they have basis. When converting, they cannot choose to convert only the basis: the taxable portion is the ratio of pre-tax money to total money across all of their Traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs (at every firm) as of December 31 of the conversion year. See The "backdoor Roth" strategy.

Withholding

We do not withhold tax from conversions by default. A client may elect withholding, but the withheld amount is a distribution (not converted) and may be subject to the 10% penalty if they are under 59½. Most clients pay conversion tax from other funds or through estimated tax payments. Explain these facts; do not advise.

Reporting

  • Form 1099-R from Bob's for the Traditional or SEP IRA, code 2 or 7 (depending on age), showing the conversion as a distribution.
  • Form 5498 for the Roth IRA, showing the conversion amount.
  • The client reports the conversion on IRS Form 8606.

Effects beyond income tax

Additional income can affect other things: Medicare premiums (IRMAA, based on income two years earlier), taxation of Social Security benefits, eligibility for credits, and state taxes. Mention that conversions can have these effects and that a tax advisor can review them.
