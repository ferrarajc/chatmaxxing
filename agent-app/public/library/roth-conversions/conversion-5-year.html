The five-year rule for conversions

Section: Roth Conversions · Page ID: conversion-5-year

Why each conversion has its own five-year clock for penalty purposes and how it affects clients under 59½.

On this page

  • The rule
  • Examples
  • Separate from the qualified-distribution clock
  • In chat
  • Why the rule exists
  • Tracking

The rule

Each conversion starts its own five-year period, beginning January 1 of the year of the conversion. If the client withdraws converted money (under the Roth ordering rules) before that period ends and before age 59½, the 10% penalty applies to the portion of the conversion that was taxable, unless an exception applies.

Examples

  • A 45-year-old converts $20,000 in 2026. If they withdraw it in 2028, the penalty may apply. In 2031 or later, no penalty.
  • A 62-year-old converts in 2026 and withdraws in 2027: no penalty because they are over 59½. (Earnings withdrawn may still be taxable if the qualified-distribution test is not met.)

Separate from the qualified-distribution clock

This conversion clock is different from the clock that determines whether earnings are tax-free. See Roth five-year rules.

In chat

If a client under 59½ asks about converting and then using the money soon, explain the rule factually and suggest a tax advisor.

Why the rule exists

Without it, someone under 59½ could avoid the early-withdrawal penalty by converting and immediately withdrawing. The five-year wait closes that loophole.

Tracking

Each conversion's year appears on the client's Form 5498 for the Roth IRA. Clients should keep these to track their conversion clocks.
