Beneficiaries: overview

Section: Beneficiaries · Page ID: beneficiaries-overview

Why beneficiary designations matter, which accounts have them, how they interact with a will, and how clients manage them.

On this page

  • Why they matter
  • Which accounts
  • Each account is separate
  • Where clients manage them
  • If there is no beneficiary
  • What you may say

Why they matter

A beneficiary designation tells us who receives an account when the owner dies. For IRAs and accounts with transfer-on-death (TOD) registration, the designation overrides the client's will: the assets go directly to the named beneficiaries without probate. An outdated designation can send money to an ex-spouse or a person who has died. Encourage clients to review beneficiaries after any major life event. See Reviewing beneficiaries after life events.

Which accounts

AccountBeneficiaries
Traditional, Roth, SEP IRAsYes, designated per account
Inherited IRAYes (successor beneficiaries)
Individual taxableOptional, through TOD registration. See Transfer on death (TOD) for taxable accounts
Joint taxable (JTWROS)Optional TOD, effective after the last owner dies
Custodial (UTMA/UGMA)No; the account belongs to the minor and passes under state law
TrustNo; the trust document governs

Each account is separate

Beneficiaries are designated separately for each account. A client with a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA must name beneficiaries on each. Ask which account(s) a change applies to.

Where clients manage them

Online at My Account → Beneficiaries, or through chat with the Change Beneficiary Designations task (see Procedure: Change Beneficiary Designations (chat)). Some designations (trusts with complex terms, designations with conditions) require a paper form.

If there is no beneficiary

If an IRA owner dies with no living beneficiary named, the IRA's governing agreement directs payment to the surviving spouse, or if none, the estate. Estate beneficiaries have less favorable distribution options. This is a good reason to name beneficiaries, which you may point out.

What you may say

You can explain how designations work. You cannot advise whom to name or how to divide the account. For estate planning advice, suggest an estate planning attorney.
