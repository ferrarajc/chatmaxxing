Trusts, estates, and charities as beneficiaries

Section: Beneficiaries · Page ID: trusts-estates-as-beneficiaries

Naming a trust, the estate, or a charity as beneficiary, and the distribution consequences for IRAs.

Trusts

A client can name a trust as beneficiary. We need the trust's exact name, date, trustee name(s), and tax ID. For IRAs, how the trust is treated depends on whether it qualifies as a "see-through" trust, which depends on the trust's terms. Distribution rules can be complex. Refer the client to their estate planning attorney, and route any detailed questions to Retirement Specialists.

The estate

Naming "my estate" is allowed. For IRAs, an estate is not a designated beneficiary, so the distribution options are less favorable (generally the 5-year rule or the owner's remaining life expectancy). The assets also go through probate. Explain these facts if the client asks.

Charities

Charities can be named as full or partial beneficiaries. Because charities do not pay income tax, naming a charity as IRA beneficiary is common in charitable planning. We need the charity's legal name and tax ID. A charity named alongside individuals can complicate the individuals' options unless its share is paid out promptly after death; Estate Services handles this.

Processing

Trust and charity beneficiaries can be added online or through the chat task (relationship "trust" or "charity"). Complex trust provisions may require the paper form.

Example designation

"The Lee Family Trust dated March 14, 2019, Margaret Lee, Trustee — 100% primary."

Charities

"Philadelphia Children's Hospital Foundation, EIN 23-XXXXXXX — 10% primary."

Common question

"Should I name my trust or my children?"

That's an estate planning decision. An estate planning attorney can help.
