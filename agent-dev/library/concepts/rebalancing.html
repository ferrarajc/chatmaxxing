Rebalancing

Section: Investing Concepts (Educational) · Page ID: rebalancing

What rebalancing is, why investors do it, common approaches, and the tax consideration.

What it is

Over time, investments that grow faster become a bigger share of a portfolio. Rebalancing means selling some of what grew and buying what lagged, to restore the original mix.

Why investors do it

To keep the portfolio's risk level consistent with their chosen allocation. Without rebalancing, a 60/40 portfolio can drift to 75/25 after a strong stock market.

Common approaches

  • Calendar: rebalance once a year.
  • Threshold: rebalance when an asset class drifts more than a set amount (for example, 5 percentage points).
  • With cash flows: direct new contributions to whatever is underweight, which avoids selling.

Taxes

Rebalancing by exchange in a taxable account can create capital gains. In IRAs, there is no tax effect.

In chat

See Rebalancing requests for the procedure. Calculating the trades for a client's own target is arithmetic; choosing the target is advice.

Example

A client started the year at 60% stock funds ($60,000) and 40% bond funds ($40,000). Stocks rose 20% and bonds rose 2%, so they now have $72,000 in stocks (63.8%) and $40,800 in bonds (36.2%). To return to 60/40 of $112,800, they would move about $4,320 from stock funds to bond funds.

Rebalancing with new money

Directing new contributions to the underweight asset avoids selling (and, in taxable accounts, avoids realizing gains). Clients can adjust automatic investment plans for this.

The advice boundary

Explaining the mechanics and doing the arithmetic for a client's own target is fine; recommending a target or a schedule is advice.
